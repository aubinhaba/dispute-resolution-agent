title: Dispute and Representment Deadlines
---

## Why deadlines decide cases

A large share of disputes are lost on the calendar rather than on the facts. A
representment submitted one day late is rejected without the evidence being read.
Any assessment of a dispute must therefore establish the remaining time before it
considers the strength of the evidence.

## Issuer dispute windows

Both networks allow the issuer 120 calendar days from the transaction processing
date for cardholder-driven disputes. Where the claim concerns goods or services
to be delivered later, the clock starts at the expected delivery or performance
date instead, subject to an absolute limit of 540 calendar days from the
transaction. Authorisation-process disputes carry shorter windows.

## Merchant representment windows

Visa allows the merchant 30 calendar days from the dispute date to submit
representment evidence. Mastercard allows the acquirer 45 calendar days for a
second presentment. Applying one network's window to the other is a common and
expensive error, because the shorter window is the one that expires unnoticed.

## Counting the days

Days are calendar days, not business days, and include weekends and public
holidays. The count starts the day after the triggering event. Where a deadline
falls on a non-processing day, it is not extended.

## Effect of a missed deadline

A missed issuer deadline makes the dispute invalid and the merchant may have it
reversed on procedure alone. A missed merchant deadline ends the merchant's
right to represent, and the funds remain with the cardholder regardless of the
evidence that could have been produced.

## Escalation stages

After representment, the issuer may escalate to pre-arbitration and then to
arbitration. Each stage carries its own window and its own fees, and the fees can
exceed the disputed amount on low-value transactions. This is why a low-value
dispute with strong evidence is often still best accepted.
